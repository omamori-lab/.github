# The name

omamori is named after the omamori, a protective amulet, as a metaphor for
tools that protect people from harm caused by AI systems.

- An omamori is a talisman in a small cloth bag, usually brocade, carried on
  the person. It began with onmyōdō priests and Buddhist temples; shrines
  later issued it too
  ([Okada, "Shinsatsu, Mamorifuda"](https://d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/eos/detail/?id=9633)).
- Carrying it is a way to receive a kami's protection. It is returned to a
  shrine to be burned at the year's end, though it may be kept until a wish is
  granted ([Jinja Honchō, section 「身に着けてご加護を戴くお守り」](https://www.jinjahoncho.or.jp/omairi/omamori/)).
- It is treated as a sacred object, so throwing it away is discouraged
  ([Japan Tourism Agency](https://www.mlit.go.jp/tagengo-db/en/H30-00525.html)).
- In a small survey, 17 of 20 university students carried an omamori, broadly
  defined, yet over half of them did not believe in gods; more were gifts than
  purchases, mostly from parents and grandparents. The author reads carrying
  one as a way of communicating care, not only a religious act
  ([Arakawa, 2004, pp. 52–53](https://doi.org/10.24534/amjspp.13.0_52)).
- Many shrines and temples draw visitors with their omamori
  ([Matsubayashi, Tanaka and Kitani, 2020, abstract](https://doi.org/10.11247/jssd.67.0_278)).

We take the name in that sense: something given to keep people safe. We have
no connection with any shrine or religious practice, mean the name with
deep respect for the tradition, and use no shrine seals, kami names or torii.
Corrections are welcome.
